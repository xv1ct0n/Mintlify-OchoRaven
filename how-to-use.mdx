CyberLearn Hub is designed to be navigated intentionally, not browsed at random. The learners who get the most out of it are the ones who arrive with a sense of where they are, where they want to go, and a willingness to commit to a direction rather than endlessly re-evaluating options. This page walks you through how the site is structured, what the labels mean, and how to translate a browse session into an actual study plan.

## Navigating the Site

The sidebar organizes content into two primary tracks.

**Resources** groups training by type and cost — Free Training, Paid Training, Certifications, and Practice Labs. Use this track when you have a budget decision to make or when you're looking for a specific kind of learning experience (e.g., a structured video course vs. a hands-on lab environment).

**By Domain** groups training by technical specialization — Network Security, Web Application Security, Cloud Security, Penetration Testing, and others. Use this track when you already have foundational knowledge and want to go deep in a particular area or align your learning with a specific job role.

## Understanding the Labels

Every resource listing in CyberLearn Hub uses a consistent set of icons and labels so you can quickly filter what's relevant to you without reading every description in full.

| Label | Icon | What It Means |
|---|---|---|
| Free | 🆓 | No cost to access the core content |
| Paid | 💳 | Requires purchase, subscription, or exam fee |
| Beginner | 🟢 | Assumes little to no prior security knowledge |
| Intermediate | 🟡 | Assumes foundational knowledge and some hands-on experience |
| Advanced | 🔴 | Assumes professional-level skills and significant prior study |

Resources can carry multiple labels — a platform might be 💳 Paid and offer both 🟢 Beginner and 🟡 Intermediate content tracks, for example.

## Building Your Study Plan

A study plan turns a list of resources into a structured schedule. Follow these steps to build one that actually works.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Find Your Level on the Learning Paths Page">
    Head to [Learning Paths](/learning-paths) and read through the Beginner, Intermediate, and Advanced tracks. Pick the one that honestly reflects where you are today — not where you hope to be in six months. The path will tell you what topics to prioritize and in what order.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Pick One or Two Primary Platforms">
    Resist the urge to subscribe to five platforms at once. Choose one primary learning platform that covers your target level well, and optionally one supplementary resource for variety. Depth beats breadth at every stage of learning.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Add a Certification as Your Goal">
    Certifications give your study plan a concrete endpoint. Browse [Certifications](/certifications/entry-level) and pick one exam that aligns with your current level and your career target. Structure your platform choices around the exam objectives.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Supplement With CTF Practice">
    Theory without practice fades fast. Add at least one [CTF or lab platform](/practice/capture-the-flag) to your weekly routine — even two to three hours of hands-on work per week makes a measurable difference in how quickly concepts stick.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  Balance theory and practice throughout your learning, not just at the end. Every major concept you study in a course should be reinforced by a lab exercise or CTF challenge within the same week. Learners who do this consistently progress significantly faster than those who save all the hands-on work for "later."
</Tip>

## Frequently Asked Questions

<Accordion title="How do I know which resources are up to date?">
  Each resource listing includes a last-reviewed date and a note if the content covers a time-sensitive topic like a specific exam version or a rapidly evolving technology. If you spot a resource that appears outdated, the feedback link at the bottom of every page lets you flag it for review. As a general rule, check the publication date of any course before enrolling — cybersecurity content older than two to three years may reference deprecated tools or superseded exam objectives.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Are free resources as good as paid ones?">
  For foundational and intermediate learning, yes — often genuinely comparable. Professor Messer's free CompTIA study materials, TryHackMe's free tier, and the OWASP documentation are all outstanding resources that cost nothing. Where paid resources tend to pull ahead is in structured lab environments, mentorship or community access, official exam prep materials, and advanced offensive security content. The honest answer is: start free, and only pay when you hit a ceiling that free content can't get you past.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="How long does it take to get job-ready?">
  It depends heavily on your starting point and how many hours per week you study. A complete beginner studying 10–15 hours per week can realistically reach an entry-level security analyst role within 12–18 months with the right path and a CompTIA Security+ or equivalent certification. Someone with existing IT or networking experience may reach the same point in 6–9 months. Treat any timeline you see — including the ones on this site — as an estimate, not a guarantee.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Should I get certified before applying for jobs?">
  For entry-level roles, a certification like CompTIA Security+, Google Cybersecurity Certificate, or eJPT meaningfully improves your resume screening rate, especially if you don't have prior security work experience. It's not strictly required, but it signals baseline competency to hiring managers who see hundreds of applications. For mid-level and senior roles, demonstrated experience and portfolio work (lab writeups, CTF achievements, open-source contributions) tend to carry more weight than certifications alone.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="What's the best first certification?">
  For most beginners, **CompTIA Security+** is the strongest first certification. It's vendor-neutral, widely recognized, DoD-approved, and covers the broad foundational knowledge that employers expect at the entry level. If you're on a tight budget, the **Google Cybersecurity Certificate** (available on Coursera) is a lower-cost alternative that covers similar ground and is increasingly accepted by entry-level employers. If you're leaning toward a penetration testing career specifically, the **eJPT** (eLearnSecurity Junior Penetration Tester) is an affordable, practical first step before pursuing OSCP.
</Accordion>
